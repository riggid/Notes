# Unit 2: Engineering Materials, Stress Analysis, and Power Transmission

## Introduction to Engineering Materials

**Engineering materials** are substances manufactured and used for various engineering applications. Studying materials is crucial for engineers to select the right material with the appropriate combination of characteristics (properties, cost, durability) for a specific design problem, considering in-service conditions and potential deterioration.

---
## Classification of Materials

Solid materials are generally classified into:

1.  **Metals**: Characterized by metallic bonding, good electrical and thermal conductivity, strength, stiffness, ductility. Often alloyed.
    * **Ferrous**: Iron (Fe) is the main constituent (e.g., steels, cast irons).
    * **Non-ferrous**: Base metal is not iron (e.g., aluminum, copper, titanium alloys).
2.  **Ceramics**: Compounds between metallic and non-metallic elements (often oxides, nitrides, carbides). Typically hard, brittle, insulating, and resistant to high temperatures and harsh environments.
3.  **Polymers (Plastics)**: Organic compounds based on carbon, hydrogen, and other non-metallic elements. Large molecular structures, often low density, flexible, and insulating.
    * **Thermoplastics**: Soften upon heating, harden upon cooling (reversible).
    * **Thermosetting plastics**: Harden permanently upon heating.
4.  **Composites**: Materials made from two or more constituent materials (often from the above categories) which remain distinct at the macroscopic level. Designed to achieve a combination of properties (e.g., strength and lightness).
    * **Matrix Phase**: The continuous material in which the reinforcement is embedded.
    * **Reinforcing Phase**: Fibers, particles, or flakes embedded in the matrix to improve properties.
5.  **Advanced Materials**: Materials used in high-tech applications.
    * Semiconductors, Biomaterials, Smart Materials, Nanomaterials.

---
## Metals and Alloys

### Ferrous Alloys (Iron-based)

* Main alloying element is **Carbon (C)**.
* **Steels**: Iron alloys typically containing less than 1.5% Carbon.
    * **Mild Steels (Low-Carbon)**: 0.15-0.25% C. Moderate strength, good weldability, low cost.
    * **Medium Carbon Steels**: 0.3-0.6% C. Higher strength, less weldable than mild steel.
    * **High Carbon Steels**: 0.65-1.5% C. Hard and tough after heat treatment, poor weldability. Used for tools, springs.
    * **Plain Carbon Steel**: Contains Carbon (up to 1.5%), Silicon (up to 0.5%), Manganese (up to 1.5%), with traces of others.
    * **Alloy Steels**: Contain additional elements (e.g., Chromium, Nickel, Molybdenum) to enhance specific properties.
        * **Tool Steels**: Designed for cutting tools, dies. High hardness, wear resistance, retain hardness at high temperatures. Alloyed with Cr, V, Mo, W.         * **Stainless Steels**: Contain at least 10.5% Chromium (Cr), forming a passive oxide layer for excellent corrosion resistance. Other elements (Ni, Mo) can be added. Used in architecture, food industry, medical tools, automotive. * **Cast Irons**: Iron alloys typically containing 2-4% Carbon. Lower cost, good for casting complex shapes.
    * **Grey Cast Iron**: Carbon exists as graphite flakes. Grey fracture surface. Weak and brittle in tension, good damping, wear resistant. Used for machine bases, engine blocks.     * **White Cast Iron**: Carbon exists as iron carbide (cementite). White fracture surface. Very hard, brittle, difficult to machine. Used for wear-resistant parts (e.g., rolling mill rollers). Often heat-treated to make malleable iron.     * **Nodular (Ductile) Cast Iron**: Adding Mg/Cr to grey iron melt forms graphite nodules (spheres). Stronger and more ductile than grey cast iron. Used for pump bodies, crankshafts.     * **Malleable Cast Iron**: Produced by heat treating white cast iron. Cementite decomposes into ferrite and graphite clusters (rosettes). Stronger and more ductile than white or grey iron. Used for connecting rods, heavy-duty parts. 
### Non-Ferrous Alloys

Used when properties like lower density, higher conductivity, or better corrosion resistance are needed compared to ferrous alloys.
* **Aluminum (Al) Alloys**: Low density, high thermal/electrical conductivity, good corrosion resistance, ductile (FCC structure). Low melting point limits high-temperature use. Alloyed with Cu, Si, Mg, Zn, Li. Used in aircraft structures, automotive parts, beverage cans. * **Copper (Cu) Alloys**: Excellent electrical conductivity, good corrosion resistance. Includes brass (Cu-Zn) and bronze (Cu-Sn). Used in wiring, plumbing, heat exchangers. * **Magnesium (Mg) Alloys**: Very low density (lightest structural metal), good machinability. Used where weight saving is critical (aerospace, automotive). Lower strength and corrosion resistance compared to Al.
* **Titanium (Ti) Alloys**: High strength-to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance, retains strength at high temperatures. More expensive. Used in aerospace, medical implants, chemical processing.
* **Other**: Zinc (Zn), Nickel (Ni), Lead (Pb), Tin (Sn) alloys etc.

---
## Ceramics

Compounds of metallic and non-metallic elements (oxides, nitrides, carbides) with ionic/covalent bonding.
* **Properties**: High hardness, high compressive strength (but low tensile strength/brittle), high temperature resistance, good insulation (thermal & electrical), chemically inert.
* **Examples**: Alumina ($Al_2O_3$), Silicon Carbide (SiC), Zirconia ($ZrO_2$), Tungsten Carbide (WC), Magnesia (MgO).
* **Applications**: Cutting tools, abrasives, thermal insulation (furnace linings, refractories), spark plugs, electronic substrates, turbine blades, bearings. 
---
## Polymers (Plastics)

Organic materials with long molecular chains (polymers), primarily based on Carbon.
* **Properties**: Low density, generally flexible, good electrical/thermal insulation, easy to manufacture into complex shapes, varying strength and temperature resistance.
* **Types**:
    * **Thermoplastics**: Soften when heated, harden when cooled (recyclable). Examples: Polyethylene (PE - pipes, bottles), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC - pipes, window frames), Nylon (Polyamide - gears, bearings), Teflon (PTFE - non-stick coatings, bearings).
    * **Thermosetting Plastics (Thermosets)**: Harden permanently during processing (cannot be remelted). Generally stronger and more rigid than thermoplastics. Examples: Epoxy (adhesives, composites), Phenolic (brake linings, electrical components), Polyester (boat hulls).
* **Applications**: Packaging, textiles, automotive parts (bumpers, interiors), pipes, electrical insulation, gears, bearings, medical devices. 
---
## Other Material Classes

### Abrasives

Hard, sharp materials used to wear, grind, or cut other materials. Can be natural or synthetic.
* **Natural**: Diamond, Corundum ($Al_2O_3$), Garnet, Emery.
* **Synthetic**: Silicon Carbide (SiC), Synthetic Aluminum Oxide ($Al_2O_3$), Cubic Boron Nitride (CBN), Synthetic Diamond.
* **Properties**: High hardness, toughness (resistance to fracture).
* **Applications**: Grinding wheels, sandpaper, polishing compounds, cutting tools. 
### Glass

Amorphous (non-crystalline) solid, primarily silica ($SiO_2$) with additives (e.g., soda, lime, lead oxide, boron oxide).
* **Properties**: Transparent, hard, brittle, chemically inert, good electrical insulator. Thermal resistance varies (Borosilicate glass is heat resistant).
* **Types**: Soda-lime glass (windows, bottles), Lead glass (crystalware), Borosilicate glass (labware, cookware).
* **Applications**: Windows, containers, lenses, optical fibers, lab equipment. 
---
## Composites

Materials made from two or more distinct constituent materials combined at a macroscopic level. Goal is to achieve properties superior to individual components.
* **Reinforcing Phase**: Provides strength and stiffness (fibers, particles, flakes).
* **Matrix Phase**: Surrounds and binds the reinforcement, transfers load (polymer, metal, ceramic). 
### Advantages over Metals:

* Higher strength-to-weight ratio.
* Higher stiffness-to-weight ratio.
* Improved fatigue and impact resistance.
* Better corrosion resistance.
* Tailorable properties (e.g., low thermal expansion).
* Potential for part consolidation (reducing assembly needs).

### Classification based on Reinforcement:

* **Particulate Composites**: Reinforcement is particles (e.g., concrete, Carbon black in rubber).
* **Flake Composites**: Reinforcement is flat flakes.
* **Fiber Composites (FRCs)**: Reinforcement is fibers (long or short). Strongest and stiffest type.

### Classification based on Matrix:

* **Polymer Matrix Composites (PMCs)**: Most common. Polymer matrix (epoxy, polyester) reinforced with fibers (glass, carbon, aramid/Kevlar). E.g., Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP), Glass Fiber Reinforced Polymer (GFRP).
    * **Applications**: Aerospace structures, automotive body panels, sporting goods (bikes, rackets), boat hulls. * **Metal Matrix Composites (MMCs)**: Metal matrix (Al, Mg, Ti) reinforced with fibers or particles (SiC, $Al_2O_3$, Carbon). Better high-temperature performance and toughness than PMCs.
    * **Applications**: Aerospace components, automotive engine parts (pistons, cylinder liners), brake rotors, electronic heat sinks. * **Ceramic Matrix Composites (CMCs)**: Ceramic matrix (SiC, $Al_2O_3$) reinforced with fibers (SiC, Carbon). Overcomes brittleness of monolithic ceramics, used in very high-temperature applications.
    * **Applications**: Gas turbine engine components, heat shields, brake systems.

---
## Smart Materials

Materials that sense changes in their environment (stimuli) and respond in a useful, predictable way. The response is often a change in shape, properties, or energy state.
* **Stimuli**: Temperature, stress, strain, electric field, magnetic field, light, pH.
* **Smart Structure/System**: Integrates smart materials with sensors, actuators, and control units to create adaptive systems. 
### Types of Smart Materials:

* **Piezoelectric Materials**: Generate electric charge when mechanically stressed (direct effect), and change shape when an electric field is applied (converse effect).
    * **Materials**: Quartz, PZT (Lead Zirconate Titanate), PVDF (Polyvinylidene Fluoride).
    * **Applications**: Sensors (pressure, acceleration), actuators (precision positioning, vibration control), energy harvesting, ultrasound transducers. * **Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs)**: Can be deformed at a low temperature (martensite phase) and recover their original shape upon heating above a transformation temperature (austenite phase) - **Shape Memory Effect (SME)**.
    * **One-Way SME**: Recovers shape upon heating, requires external force to deform again upon cooling.
    * **Two-Way SME**: Remembers shapes in both high and low temperature phases, cycling between them with temperature change.
    * **Materials**: Nitinol (Ni-Ti), Cu-Al-Ni, Cu-Zn-Al.
    * **Applications**: Medical (stents, orthodontic wires), actuators (aerospace, automotive), temperature-sensitive valves, couplings. * **Rheological Fluids**: Fluids whose viscosity changes significantly and reversibly when exposed to an electric or magnetic field.
    * **Electrorheological (ER) Fluids**: Respond to electric fields. Contain dielectric particles in non-conducting oil.
    * **Magnetorheological (MR) Fluids**: Respond to magnetic fields. Contain ferromagnetic particles in oil.
    * **Working Principle**: Field causes particles to align into chains, resisting flow/shear. Effect is rapid and controllable. [Image showing rheological fluid particle alignment]
    * **Applications**: Controllable dampers (vehicle suspension, seismic protection), clutches, brakes, prosthetic devices. * **Other Types**: Electrostrictive (shape change in electric field), Magnetostrictive (shape change in magnetic field), Thermoresponsive (change properties with temp), Electrochromic (change color with voltage).

## Introduction to Mechanics of Materials

Branch of applied mechanics dealing with the behavior of solid bodies under loads. Aims to determine **stress**, **strain**, and **displacement** within structures to ensure safe design.

---
## Normal Stress and Strain

* **Prismatic Bar**: Straight member with constant cross-section.
* **Axial Load (P)**: Force acting along the axis, causing tension or compression.
* **Normal Stress ($\sigma$)**: Intensity of internal force acting perpendicular to a surface. Calculated as force per unit area.
    $$\sigma = \frac{P}{A}$$
    * Units: Pascals (Pa = N/m²) or psi (lb/in²).
    * **Tensile Stress**: Positive, due to pulling force.
    * **Compressive Stress**: Negative, due to pushing force.
    * **Nominal Stress (Engineering Stress)**: Calculated using the original cross-sectional area $A_0$.
    * **True Stress**: Calculated using the actual, instantaneous cross-sectional area $A$.
* **Normal Strain ($\epsilon$)**: Measure of deformation (elongation or shortening) per unit length.
    $$\epsilon = \frac{\delta}{L}$$
    * $\delta$ = change in length, L = original length.
    * Dimensionless (m/m or in/in). Often expressed as percentage or microstrain ($\mu\epsilon$).
    * **Tensile Strain**: Positive (elongation).
    * **Compressive Strain**: Negative (shortening).
    * **Nominal Strain (Engineering Strain)**: Calculated using original length $L_0$.
    * **True Strain (Natural Strain)**: Calculated based on instantaneous length.

---
## Stress-Strain Diagram

A plot of stress versus strain obtained from material testing (e.g., tensile test on a Universal Testing Machine - UTM). Characterizes the material's mechanical behavior.

### Stress-Strain Diagram for Mild Steel (Ductile Material)

* **O-A (Linear Elastic Region)**: Stress is directly proportional to strain (obeys Hooke's Law). Slope is the **Modulus of Elasticity (E)**. Ends at the **Proportional Limit (A)**.
* **A-B (Non-linear Elastic Region)**: Stress is no longer proportional to strain, but deformation is still elastic (material returns to original shape if load removed). Ends at the **Elastic Limit**. (Often very close to A).
* **B (Yield Point)**: Stress at which the material begins to deform plastically (permanently) with little or no increase in load. Often exhibits upper and lower yield points. Stress at B is the **Yield Stress ($\sigma_y$)**.
* **B-C (Yielding / Perfect Plasticity)**: Material elongates significantly with little or no increase in stress.
* **C-D (Strain Hardening)**: Material's crystalline structure changes, increasing its resistance to further deformation. Stress increases with increasing strain.
* **D (Ultimate Tensile Strength - UTS)**: Maximum stress the material can withstand on the engineering stress-strain curve. Necking (localized reduction in cross-section) begins here. Stress is the **Ultimate Stress ($\sigma_u$)**.
* **D-E (Necking)**: Cross-sectional area decreases significantly in a localized region. Engineering stress decreases, but true stress continues to increase.
* **E (Fracture Point)**: Material breaks. Stress at E is the **Fracture Stress**.

### Offset Yield Strength

For materials like aluminum alloys that don't have a distinct yield point, the **offset yield strength** (commonly 0.2% offset = 0.002 strain) is defined. A line parallel to the initial elastic slope is drawn from the offset strain value on the strain axis. The stress where this line intersects the curve is taken as the yield strength.

### Ductility Measures

Quantify the amount of plastic deformation a material can undergo before fracture.
* **Percent Elongation**: Increase in gauge length at fracture, expressed as a percentage of the original gauge length $L_0$.
    $$\text{Percent Elongation} = \frac{L_f - L_0}{L_0} \times 100\%$$
    ($L_f$ = length at fracture). Depends on gauge length.
* **Percent Reduction in Area**: Decrease in cross-sectional area at fracture, expressed as a percentage of the original area $A_0$. Measures necking.
    $$\text{Percent Reduction in Area} = \frac{A_0 - A_f}{A_0} \times 100\%$$
    ($A_f$ = area at fracture).

### Brittle Materials

Materials like cast iron, glass, concrete that exhibit little or no plastic deformation before fracture. Stress-strain curve is often nearly linear up to fracture. Fracture stress is often the same as ultimate stress. Tensile strength is much lower than compressive strength.

### Compression Tests

Stress-strain behavior in compression can differ from tension.
* **Ductile Materials**: Do not typically fracture in compression. They bulge outwards (barreling) and offer increasing resistance. Yield strengths in tension and compression are usually similar. * **Brittle Materials**: Fracture in compression, often at much higher stresses than their tensile strength.

---
## Linear Elasticity and Hooke's Law

In the initial linear elastic region, stress is proportional to strain.
* **Hooke's Law (Normal Stress)**:
    $$\sigma = E \epsilon$$
    * **E**: **Modulus of Elasticity** or **Young's Modulus**. Measure of stiffness (resistance to elastic deformation). Units of stress (Pa or psi). Higher E means stiffer material.
* **Hooke's Law (Shear Stress)**:
    $$\tau = G \gamma$$
    * **$\tau$**: Shear stress (force parallel to area).
    * **$\gamma$**: Shear strain (change in angle).
    * **G**: **Shear Modulus** or **Modulus of Rigidity**. Measure of resistance to shear deformation.

---
## Poisson's Ratio ($\nu$)

When a material is stretched axially (tensile strain $\epsilon_{axial}$), it contracts laterally (compressive strain $\epsilon_{lateral}$). Poisson's ratio is the ratio of the magnitude of lateral strain to axial strain in the elastic region.
$$\nu = - \frac{\epsilon_{lateral}}{\epsilon_{axial}}$$
The negative sign makes $\nu$ positive, as axial and lateral strains have opposite signs.
* Dimensionless.
* Typical values for metals: 0.25 - 0.35.
* Theoretical limits: -1 to 0.5. Most engineering materials: 0 to 0.5. Rubber is close to 0.5. Cork is close to 0.

Relationship between E, G, and $\nu$:
$$G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$$

---
## Shear Stress and Strain

* **Shear Stress ($\tau$)**: Intensity of internal force acting **parallel** to a surface. Caused by shear forces (V).
    $$\tau_{avg} = \frac{V}{A}$$
    Where A is the area parallel to the force V. Example: stress in bolts, rivets, pins. * **Shear Strain ($\gamma$)**: Measure of the change in angle (distortion) of an element due to shear stress. Measured in radians. 
---
## Mechanical Properties

* **Strength**: Ability to withstand applied stress without failure (yield or fracture). (Yield Strength, Ultimate Strength).
* **Elasticity**: Ability to return to original shape after load removal.
* **Plasticity**: Ability to undergo permanent deformation without fracture.
* **Stiffness**: Ability to resist elastic deformation (related to Modulus of Elasticity E).
* **Resilience**: Ability to absorb energy during elastic deformation and release it upon unloading. **Modulus of Resilience** is the area under the elastic portion of the stress-strain curve. [Image showing modulus of resilience]
* **Toughness**: Ability to absorb energy up to fracture (combination of strength and ductility). **Modulus of Toughness** is the total area under the stress-strain curve. [Image showing modulus of toughness]
* **Ductility**: Ability to deform plastically under tensile stress before fracture (can be drawn into wires). Measured by % Elongation and % Reduction in Area.
* **Malleability**: Ability to deform plastically under compressive stress without fracture (can be hammered into thin sheets).
* **Brittleness**: Tendency to fracture with little or no plastic deformation. Opposite of ductility.
* **Hardness**: Resistance to scratching, abrasion, indentation, or penetration.

---
## Deformations Under Axial Loading

For a linearly elastic, homogeneous bar of length L, constant cross-sectional area A, and Young's Modulus E, subjected to an axial load P, the deformation (elongation or shortening) $\delta$ is:
$$\delta = \frac{PL}{AE}$$
* $\delta$ is positive for tension, negative for compression.

For a bar composed of multiple segments or with varying loads/properties:
$$\delta_{total} = \sum_{i} \delta_i = \sum_{i} \frac{P_i L_i}{A_i E_i}$$
Where $P_i$ is the **internal axial force** in segment $i$.

---
## Factor of Safety (F.S.)

A ratio used in design to ensure safety by limiting the working stress/load to a level well below the material's failure point. Accounts for uncertainties in loads, material properties, analysis methods, etc.

* Based on Load:
    $$F.S. = \frac{\text{Ultimate Load}}{\text{Allowable Load}}$$
* Based on Stress:
    $$F.S. = \frac{\text{Failure Stress}}{\text{Allowable Stress}}$$
    * Failure stress can be **Ultimate Stress ($\sigma_u$)** (for brittle materials or considering fracture) or **Yield Stress ($\sigma_y$)** (for ductile materials, to prevent permanent deformation).
    * Allowable Stress $\sigma_{allow} = \text{Failure Stress} / F.S.$

## Power Transmission Drives

Mechanisms to transmit power from a driving shaft to a driven shaft.

* **Rigid Drives**: Direct contact (e.g., Gear Drives). Precise velocity ratio.
* **Flexible Drives**: Intermediate flexible link (e.g., Belt Drives, Chain Drives). Can handle larger distances, absorb shock.

---
## Belt Drives

Transmit power between shafts (often parallel) using friction between a belt and pulleys. Consists of driving pulley, driven pulley, and an endless belt.

### Types of Belts:

* **Flat Belts**: Rectangular cross-section. Used with crowned pulleys to help tracking. Suitable for long distances. * **V-Belts**: Trapezoidal cross-section. Run in grooved pulleys. Wedge action increases friction, allowing higher power transmission and less slip than flat belts over shorter distances. More expensive. * **Round Belts**: Circular cross-section. Used for light duties, can handle drives in multiple planes (e.g., sewing machines, vacuum cleaners). 
### Types of Belt Drives:

* **Open Belt Drive**: Shafts rotate in the same direction. * **Crossed Belt Drive**: Shafts rotate in opposite directions. Belt crosses itself. Provides larger angle of wrap, potentially more power, but more belt wear. 
### Belt Drive Terminology:

* **Velocity Ratio (VR)**: Ratio of driven pulley speed ($N_2$) to driving pulley speed ($N_1$).
    * Neglecting thickness: $VR = \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{d_1}{d_2}$ (where $d_1, d_2$ are pulley diameters).
    * Including thickness (t): $VR = \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{d_1 + t}{d_2 + t}$.
* **Slip**: Relative motion between belt and pulley due to insufficient friction. Reduces velocity ratio.
    * $VR = \frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{d_1 + t}{d_2 + t} (1 - \frac{s}{100})$ (where s is total percentage slip).
* **Length of Belt**:
    * Open Belt: $L_O \approx \pi (r_1+ r_2) + 2x + \frac{(r_1- r_2)^2}{x}$
    * Crossed Belt: $L_C \approx \pi (r_1+ r_2) + 2x + \frac{(r_1+ r_2)^2}{x}$
      (where $r_1, r_2$ are radii, $x$ is center distance).
* **Angle of Contact ($\theta$)**: Angle over which the belt wraps around the pulley (in radians). Affects friction grip.
    * Open Belt (smaller pulley): $\theta = (\pi - 2\alpha)$ radians, where $\sin \alpha = (r_1-r_2)/x$.
    * Crossed Belt (both pulleys): $\theta = (\pi + 2\alpha)$ radians, where $\sin \alpha = (r_1+r_2)/x$.
* **Belt Tensions**:
    * $T_1$: Tension on the tight side (pulling side).
    * $T_2$: Tension on the slack side.
    * **Ratio of Tensions (Flat Belt)**: $\frac{T_1}{T_2} = e^{\mu \theta}$ (where $\mu$ is coefficient of friction).
    * **Ratio of Tensions (V-Belt)**: $\frac{T_1}{T_2} = e^{\mu \theta / \sin \alpha}$ (where $2\alpha$ is the groove angle). V-belts have higher tension ratio due to wedge action ($1/\sin \alpha$ term).
* **Power Transmitted (P)**:
    $$P = (T_1 - T_2) v$$
    where $v$ is the linear belt speed ($v = \pi d N / 60$).

### Belt Drive Advantages/Disadvantages:

* **Advantages**: Simple, low cost, smooth/quiet operation, absorb shock, can transmit over long distances, slip provides overload protection.
* **Disadvantages**: Lower efficiency, velocity ratio not constant (slip), larger space needed, finite belt life, high loads on shafts/bearings.

## Gear Drives

Transmit power between shafts using meshing toothed wheels (gears). Provide a positive drive with a constant velocity ratio. Suitable for high power, compact designs. 
### Classification of Gears (based on shaft axes):

1.  **Parallel Shafts**:
    * **Spur Gears**: Straight teeth parallel to shaft axis. Simple, economical. Can be noisy at high speeds. External meshing reverses direction; internal meshing keeps same direction.     * **Helical Gears**: Teeth are angled (helix). Gradual engagement, smoother, quieter, higher load capacity than spur gears. Produce axial thrust.     * **Double Helical / Herringbone Gears**: Two sets of opposite-hand helical teeth. Cancel out axial thrust. Used for high power/speed. Herringbone gears have no central groove. 2.  **Intersecting Shafts**:
    * **Straight Bevel Gears**: Teeth are straight but tapered on a cone shape. Connect shafts typically at $90^{\circ}$. Mitre gears are equal-sized bevel gears ($1:1$ ratio).     * **Spiral Bevel Gears**: Teeth are curved/spiral. Smoother, quieter, higher load capacity than straight bevels. Produce axial thrust. Used in differentials. 3.  **Skew Shafts (Non-intersecting, Non-parallel)**:
    * **Hypoid Gears**: Similar to spiral bevel but axes are offset. Used in automotive rear axles to lower driveshaft position.     * **Worm Gears**: Consist of a screw-like worm meshing with a worm wheel. Connect perpendicular skew shafts. Achieve very high speed reduction ratios. Often self-locking. 
### Special Case:

* **Rack and Pinion**: A spur gear (pinion) meshing with a flat toothed bar (rack). Converts rotary motion to linear motion or vice-versa. Used in steering systems, machine tool slides. 
### Gear Terminology:

[Image illustrating gear terminology on a tooth]
* **Pitch Circle**: Imaginary circle representing the equivalent friction cylinder diameter.
* **Pitch Diameter (d)**: Diameter of the pitch circle.
* **Number of Teeth (T or N)**: Total number of teeth on the gear.
* **Circular Pitch (p)**: Distance along pitch circle between corresponding points on adjacent teeth. $p = \pi d / T$.
* **Diametral Pitch (P)**: (Imperial units) Number of teeth per inch of pitch diameter. $P = T / d$. $P \times p = \pi$.
* **Module (m)**: (SI units) Ratio of pitch diameter (in mm) to number of teeth. $m = d / T$. $p = \pi m$. **Mating gears must have the same module.**
* **Addendum**: Radial height from pitch circle to top of tooth.
* **Dedendum**: Radial depth from pitch circle to bottom of tooth space.
* **Clearance**: Radial difference between dedendum of one gear and addendum of mating gear.
* **Center Distance (C)**: Distance between centers of mating gears. $C = (d_1 + d_2) / 2 = m(T_1 + T_2) / 2$.
* **Velocity Ratio (VR)** or **Gear Ratio (i)**: Ratio of input speed to output speed.
    $$VR = \frac{N_{driver}}{N_{driven}} = \frac{\omega_{driver}}{\omega_{driven}} = \frac{d_{driven}}{d_{driver}} = \frac{T_{driven}}{T_{driver}}$$

### Gear Trains

Combination of gears to transmit motion and achieve desired speed/torque changes.
* **Simple Gear Train**: Each shaft carries only one gear. Axes are fixed. Intermediate gears (idlers) affect direction but not overall speed ratio.
    $$\frac{N_{out}}{N_{in}} = (-1)^k \frac{T_{in}}{T_{out}}$$
    (k = number of external gear pairs).
    $$Speed Ratio = \frac{N_{in}}{N_{out}} = \frac{T_{out}}{T_{in}}$$ * **Compound Gear Train**: At least one shaft carries two or more gears fixed together (compound gear). Allows larger speed ratios in compact space.
    $$Speed Ratio = \frac{N_{in}}{N_{out}} = \frac{\text{Product of teeth on Driven gears}}{\text{Product of teeth on Driving gears}}$$ * **Reverted Gear Train**: Input and output shafts are coaxial. Often uses compound gears.
* **Planetary (Epicyclic) Gear Train**: At least one gear axis rotates around another central axis. Involves sun gear, planet gears, carrier, and often a ring gear. Compact, high ratio, can provide differential action.
